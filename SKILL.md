# Novel-writing methodology

A novel gets written in two very different registers, and most methodological failure comes from running them at the same time. One register produces the book: desire, conflict, causality, scene, voice, the thing that makes a reader turn the page without being told to. The other governs the project: what counts as evidence, when a decision is final, how corrections get made without eroding what the text already earned. This skill is mostly the second register — it is a governance system, deliberately, because governance is what silently fails on ambitious or research-heavy novels. It does not substitute generated prose for authorship, and it actively resists collapsing into a checklist that produces documentation instead of a better book — but it may generate disposable test material (a pilot, per Phase 5) when the author explicitly asks for one, provided that material is treated as a pilot to verdict on, not as canonical prose. Read "The one rule above the others" below before anything else — it is the guardrail against the skill's own main failure mode.

This skill emerged from documenting, in detail, the actual practice used to write one specific novel — then correcting that practice's blind spots (it under-specified dramatic architecture, character, and voice; some of its rules were sound locally but dangerous stated as universal laws) before generalizing it. Treat every rule below as something to argue with on a specific project, not scripture.

## The one rule above the others

**The methodology must produce decisions, not documentation.** A rigorous system's specific failure mode is becoming extremely good at documenting its own rigor. Before creating any register, audit, grid, or note this skill mentions, ask: *what future decision will this document let us make that we could not make as reliably without it?* If there's no answer, don't create it. This question overrides every instruction below that suggests writing something down.

This rule applies to the skill's own rules, not just to project registers. A rule that never changes what gets decided — relative to unassisted judgment — is consuming attention without earning it; see the eval-driven maintenance note at the end of this file.

## Constitution

Small number of invariants that hold across every phase. Everything else in this skill is a phase, protocol, or guardrail in service of these:

1. **Reader effect before author intent.** What a reader experiences is the fact; what the author meant to produce is a hypothesis about how to get there.
2. **Narrative experience before demonstration.** The reader must have a reason to keep reading that exists independently of any argument or thesis the book could be said to carry — but that reason is not fixed in advance. Curiosity about what happens next is one engine; voice, a relationship, a situation, a consciousness, a moral tension, or a formal pattern can each be another. This holds even — especially — for novels built on real research or a thesis-shaped premise: the ban is on the book arguing at the reader, not on any particular source of narrative pull. Establish, per project (Phase 0), what this book's actual engine is — don't assume it's plot.
3. **Observation before diagnosis.** Establish what is actually happening in the text (a count, a quote, a reader's reported experience) before proposing why, and don't let the two collapse into one step.
4. **Criteria before test.** Define what would count as pass/fail before running a pilot, an audit, or a reader test — never after seeing the result.
5. **No change without a stated reason and a testable expected gain.** Before freeze, this is a light bar: "this would be better because Y, checkable by Z" is enough — the point is banning unexamined tinkering, not banning improvement. What counts as clearing the bar tightens sharply once a manuscript is frozen: a preference or an untested idea, however good, is no longer sufficient on its own — see Phase 10's exceptional-revision path, which requires the gain, the risk to what's already been earned, and criteria for when the frozen version stays preferred, all stated and tested blind before anything changes. Don't let "no correction without a defect" (the pre-freeze-era phrasing of this rule) be read as "nothing but a defect can ever justify a post-freeze change" — that stronger claim is not this rule, and treating governance as more important than the book it protects is exactly the failure this methodology exists to prevent.
6. **Economy is a post-freeze discipline, not a drafting one.** Counting and justifying every word belongs to surgical correction of a finished text. Imposed during a first draft, it produces inhibited prose. Keep these two regimes strictly apart (see Phase 6 and Phase 10).
7. **Factual truth, once asserted as true of the real world inside the book, is non-negotiable — but locate exactly where the assertion lives before checking it.** A real-world claim can appear as the narrator's own assertion, a character's stated belief, a character's knowing lie, or a deliberately unresolved ambiguity the text never settles — only the first two are checkable-truth claims at all, and a character's false belief or lie is correct *as written* precisely when it's wrong as fact. Checking a claim against reality without first locating whose claim it is (narrator vs. character) and what kind (asserted, believed, lied, left ambiguous) produces exactly the over-zealous factual audit this doctrine is meant to prevent, not the discipline it's meant to enforce. Plausibility and dramatic effectiveness are separate, later questions (Phase 8).
8. **Generation and selection are different operations.** While generating alternatives — what could happen next, what a character could do, how a scene could go — don't require each option to justify its existence; a good set of alternatives includes some that are bad, contradictory, or mutually exclusive, and that range is the point. Apply architectural or narrative criteria only afterward, during selection. Treat this as an extension of the drafting/audit separation (Constitution item 6, Phase 6) to the exploratory work that precedes architecture and sequencing — see the exploration protocol in `references/protocols.md`.

## The literary evidence doctrine

The single most important discipline in this skill, because almost every other protocol depends on getting it right. Every **atomic** claim made about a manuscript belongs to exactly one of four categories, and the categories are not interchangeable:

| Category | Example | What it actually establishes |
|---|---|---|
| **Fact** | "this date is impossible" | Verifiable against the world outside the book |
| **Textual observation** | "this motif appears eleven times" | Verifiable by counting/searching the manuscript itself |
| **Reading effect** | "I understood here that the father already knew" | True for the reader who reports it; not automatically generalizable |
| **Causal interpretation** | "I understood that because this line over-explains" | A hypothesis about *why* the effect happened — needs its own test |

**Most real-world findings arrive as composite claims, not atomic ones — decompose before classifying, don't force-fit the whole sentence into one row.** "This scene contains three historical explanations and drags badly" is one sentence carrying two different claims: a textual observation (three explanations, countable) and a reading effect/causal interpretation (drags — someone's experience, plus an implicit theory of why). Classifying the whole sentence as one category either loses the countable part or smuggles the interpretation in as if it were as solid as the count. Break a finding into its component propositions first; only then does "which category" become an answerable question for each piece.

The dangerous move, once claims are properly atomized, is sliding from one category to the next without noticing: "three readers dropped off here" is a *reading effect* (data — three independent reports). "This scene is too long" is a *causal interpretation* (a theory about the cause). The first is close to unimpeachable; the second is one hypothesis among several (wrong POV, misplaced information, weak stakes, bad prose rhythm — any of these could produce the same drop-off) and must be tested before being acted on. Every audit and every procès in this skill exists partly to enforce this distinction. When reporting a finding — your own or a reader's — decompose it, then name each atomic claim's category before proposing what to do about it.

## How to use this skill

1. **Locate the project.** Ask, or infer from context, which phase the project is actually in (see the table below) — not which phase the user *thinks* they're in. A user asking for a "beta read" checklist for an unfrozen, unaudited first draft is not ready for Phase 9; say so.
2. **Read `references/phases.md`** for the phase in question before acting — it has the entry/exit conditions, the allowed registers, and what you as the assistant should and should not do at that phase. Don't rely on the compressed table below for anything operational.
3. **Apply the relevant protocol** from `references/protocols.md` rather than improvising a bespoke process each time — the protocols encode corrections to mistakes already made once (see the note on each one about which "trap" it prevents).
4. **Before writing anything to a file**, run it through the one rule above ("what decision does this enable?") and through the evidence doctrine (is this fact, observation, effect, or interpretation — and does the file say which?).
5. **Never silently overwrite a rejected finding or an earlier note that turns out to be wrong.** Strike it through and correct it in place with a visible note of the correction. The record of having been wrong once has its own value — see `references/registers.md`.
6. **Apply the reasoning; don't narrate the scaffolding.** Use the phases and protocols to decide what to say, not as vocabulary to say it in. A response built on Phase 2's two-part test should explain *why* a contemplative scene might matter without necessarily saying "Phase 2's two-part test" to do it — name a phase, a protocol, or "the Constitution" only when the user is working with the methodology explicitly (e.g. asking how the skill itself works) or when the precise term genuinely carries information a plain restatement would lose. Citing internal machinery as the explanation, by default, is its own version of the reader sensing the machine.

## The phases (state machine, compressed — full detail in `references/phases.md`)

Phases are usually sequential but not strictly linear: a project can and should move backward when a later phase produces a signal serious enough to reopen an earlier one (see each phase's reopening criteria in the reference file). Three explicit exceptions to strict sequencing, corrected from an earlier, too-rigid version of this method:

- **Research (Phase 1) and pilot scenes (Phase 5) can run concurrently.** Separate the *functions* of exploration and canonical drafting, not the calendar — some novels only discover their real question by being drafted. Don't block drafting on "finishing" research; block *canonical* drafting on having enough.
- **Chapter/beat sequencing (Phase 4) and pilot scenes (Phase 5) can run concurrently.** A unit's sequencing questions and a pilot testing that unit's specific mechanism inform each other — don't force a finished sequencing pass before any pilot can begin.
- **Early, narrow external testing (part of Phase 9) can happen before the internal audits of Phase 7-8 are exhausted**, specifically for defects the author structurally cannot self-diagnose: boredom, confusion, failure to attach to a character. Reserve broad beta reads for after the internal audits, but a single trusted early reader testing one specific mechanism is legitimate at almost any point after Phase 5.

| # | Phase | Core question it answers |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | Project contract | What is this book trying to *do* to a reader, and what stays deliberately undecided for now? |
| 1 | Research & world | What must be true, understood, plausible, or — critically — known but never shown? |
| 2 | Dramatic architecture | If this scene disappeared, what becomes impossible later — and if nothing, what becomes meaningfully different for the reader? |
| 3 | Characters | What does each person want *now*, refuse to see, know, wrongly believe, hide, and do under pressure? |
| 4 | Chapter/beat sequencing | Which beat lives in this unit, what ordinary life surrounds it, and where does it stop announcing its own structure? |
| 5 | Pilot scenes | Does this specific narrative mechanism work, tested cheaply and disposably? |
| 6 | Canonical drafting | Write the scene. Do not audit the scene while writing it. |
| 7 | Narrative audits | Causality, character, information-flow, tension, rhythm, artificiality, continuity — each a separate pass. |
| 8 | Documentary/factual audit | Is it true, is it plausible, is it internally coherent, and is it *used well* — four separate questions. |
| 9 | External readers | What did a reader experience, not what would they change. |
| 10 | Freeze & procès | The manuscript is done. Every further change is an objectifiable error, a fully-run procès, or an exceptional revision that has earned reopening. |
| 11 | Production | Typography, formats, packaging — never touches the frozen text. |

## Four corrections already built into this version

These four points come from a critique of an earlier draft of this method and are worth stating explicitly, because the failure mode they prevent is subtle — a rule that was locally excellent on one project, promoted to a universal law:

1. **"Separate research and writing" is not "never write during research."** Separate the *functions*; let pilots start early.
2. **"Exhaust the self-audit before external readers" is not a ban on early narrow reader tests.** The author is structurally the worst-positioned reader for boredom, confusion, and failure to attach — those need an early, real reader, not more self-audit.
3. **"Count and justify every word" is a post-freeze procès discipline, not a first-draft one.** Applied too early it produces inhibited, over-controlled prose. Phase 6 is explicitly protected from it.
4. **A single reader's signal can be correct about the *effect* while wrong about the *cause*.** "I stopped believing the protagonist halfway through" is a strong signal even if the reader points at the wrong scene. Never let "the reader misdiagnosed it" become a reason to dismiss what they actually reported experiencing — separate the two claims and test the effect independently of the reader's proposed cause.

## Eval-driven maintenance

`evals/results.md` is not just a validation record — it's how this skill decides what to keep. A rule earns its place in `SKILL.md` or `references/` by doing at least one of: preventing a recurring error, producing a measurably better decision than unassisted judgment (an eval discriminates for it), or protecting a load-bearing property of the system as a whole (e.g. the freeze/procès separation, even if no single eval isolates it). A rule an eval shows makes no difference (see eval 3, `evals/results.md`) is a candidate to demote to `README.md` as documented context, not to keep occupying the skill's own context on the strength of sounding right.

**Anti-evals** (evals 6-10 in `evals/evals.json`) test the complementary failure mode: not whether the skill applies its method correctly, but whether it knows when *not* to apply it. A methodology skill that always engages its machinery — audits a raw first draft on request, declares a contemplative scene structurally useless for failing the "if cut, what's impossible" test, forces closure on a valid "something's off but I can't say what" reader signal, refuses a post-freeze improvement on principle without checking whether it's actually still a non-defect, or corrects a factual error a character was written to get wrong — is failing as badly as one that never engages it at all. See `evals/results.md` for outcomes.
